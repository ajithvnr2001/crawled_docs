Cloudflare operates in [more than 275 cities in over 100 countries](https://www.cloudflare.com/network/), where we interconnect with over 10,000 network providers in order to provide a broad range of services to millions of customers. The breadth of both our network and our customer base provides us with a unique perspective on Internet resilience, enabling us to observe the impact of Internet disruptions. In many cases, these disruptions can be attributed to a physical event, while in other cases, they are due to an intentional government-directed shutdown. In this post, we review selected Internet disruptions observed by Cloudflare during the third quarter of 2022, supported by traffic graphs from [Cloudflare Radar](https://radar.cloudflare.com/) and other internal Cloudflare tools, and grouped by associated cause or common geography. The new [Cloudflare Radar Outage Center](https://radar.cloudflare.com/outage-center) provides additional information on these, and other historical, disruptions.

## Government directed shutdowns

Unfortunately, for the last decade, governments around the world have turned to shutting down the Internet as a means of controlling or limiting communication among citizens and with the outside world. In the third quarter, this was an all too popular cause of observed disruptions, impacting countries and regions in Africa, the Middle East, Asia, and the Caribbean.

### Iraq

As mentioned in our [Q2 summary blog post](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q2-2022-internet-disruption-summary/), on June 27, the Kurdistan Regional Government in Iraq began to implement twice-weekly (Mondays and Thursday) multi-hour regional Internet shutdowns over the following four weeks, intended to prevent cheating on high school final exams. As seen in the figure below, these shutdowns occurred as expected each Monday and Thursday through July 21, with the exception of July 21. ## Natural disasters

Natural disasters such as earthquakes and hurricanes wreak havoc on impacted geographies, often causing loss of life, as well as significant structural damage to buildings of all types. Infrastructure damage is also extremely common, with widespread loss of both electrical power and telecommunications infrastructure.

### Papua New Guinea

On September 11, a [7.6 magnitude earthquake struck Papua New Guinea](https://www.cnn.com/2022/09/10/asia/earthquake-papua-new-guinea), resulting in landslides, cracked roads, and Internet connectivity disruptions. Traffic to the country [dropped by 26%](https://twitter.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1569114613874102277) just after 1100 local time (0100 UTC) . The figure below shows that traffic volumes remained lower into the following day as well. An [announcement](https://subtelforum.com/png-earthquake-causing-multiple-cable-disruptions/) from PNG DataCo, a local provider, noted that the earthquake “ _has affected the operations of the Kumul Submarine Cable Network (KSCN) Express Link between Port Moresby and Madang and the PPC-1 Cable between Madang and Sydney._ ” This damage, they stated, resulted in the observed outage and degraded service.

However, later that day, another partial outage occurred, with a 50% traffic drop seen at 1330 local time (1030 UTC). This one was much shorter, with recovery starting approximately an hour later. Finally, a nominal disruption is visible at 0800 local time (0500 UTC) on September 13, with lower than expected traffic volumes lasting for around five hours.

## Cable damage

Damage to both terrestrial and submarine cables have caused many Internet disruptions over the years. The recent alleged sabotage of the sub-sea Nord Stream natural gas pipelines has brought an increasing level of interest from European media (including [Swiss](https://www.nzz.ch/technologie/nach-anschlag-auf-pipeline-europas-datenkabel-liegen-tief-im-meer-ungeschuetzt-vor-spionage-und-sabotage-ld.1705274?_x_tr_hl=) and [French](https://www.solutions-numeriques.com/apres-les-fuites-de-nord-stream-les-cables-internet-sous-marins-sont-ils-a-risques/) publications) around just how important submarine cables are to the Internet, and an increasing level of [concern among policymakers](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/IDAN/2022/702557/EXPO_IDA\(2022\)702557_EN.pdf) about the safety of these cable systems and the potential impact of damage to them. However, the three instances of cable damage reviewed below are all related to terrestrial cable.

### Iran

On [August 1](https://twitter.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1554061566059978753), a reported [“fiber optic cable” problem](https://twitter.com/SonitaSarabpour/status/1554045848689991680) caused by a [fire in a telecommunications manhole](https://twitter.com/Iran/status/1554074256127950850) disrupted connectivity across multiple network providers, including [AS31549](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as31549) (Aria Shatel), [AS58224](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as58224) (TIC), [AS43754](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as43754) (Asiatech), [AS44244](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as44244) (Irancell), and [AS197207](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as197207) (MCCI). The disruption started around 1215 local time (0845 UTC) and lasted for approximately four hours. Because it impacted a number of major wireless and wireline networks, the impact was visible at a country level as well, as seen in the figure below.

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns%3D%22http%3A%2F%2Fwww.w3.org%2F2000%2Fsvg%22%20viewBox%3D%220%200%201%201%22%3E%3Crect%20width%3D%221%22%20height%3D%221%22%20fill%3D%22rgb\(243%2C243%2C243\)%22%2F%3E%3C%2Fsvg%3E)![BLOG-1450 Embedded Image - NRTojK](https://blog.cloudflare.com/_image?href=https%3A%2F%2Fblog.cloudflare.com%2F_emdash%2Fapi%2Fmedia%2Ffile%2F01KW44ZNARVWP7PJY7WNBYDF5V.png&w=715&h=358&f=webp&fit=cover&position=center)

### Pakistan

Cable damage due to [heavy rains and flooding](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/key-pakistani-internet-providers-report-outage-2022-08-19/) caused several Internet disruptions in Pakistan in August. The first notable disruption occurred on [August 19](https://twitter.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1560585712306892800), starting around 0700 local time (0200 UTC) and lasted just over six and a half hours. On [August 22](https://twitter.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1561785560317976576), another significant disruption is also visible, starting at 2250 local time (1750 UTC), with a [further drop](https://twitter.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1561890477892407297) at 0530 local time (0030 UTC) on the 23rd. The second more significant drop was brief, lasting only 45 minutes, after which traffic began to recover.

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns%3D%22http%3A%2F%2Fwww.w3.org%2F2000%2Fsvg%22%20viewBox%3D%220%200%201%201%22%3E%3Crect%20width%3D%221%22%20height%3D%221%22%20fill%3D%22rgb\(243%2C243%2C243\)%22%2F%3E%3C%2Fsvg%3E)![BLOG-1450 Embedded Image - 949Xx2](https://blog.cloudflare.com/_image?href=https%3A%2F%2Fblog.cloudflare.com%2F_emdash%2Fapi%2Fmedia%2Ffile%2F01KW47KD4272XRN83V8FM4ZB85.png&w=715&h=268&f=webp&fit=cover&position=center)

### Haiti

Amidst protests over fuel price hikes, fiber cuts in Haiti caused Internet outages on multiple network providers. Starting at 1500 local time (1900 UTC) on [September](https://twitter.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1570389349723684865) 14, traffic on [AS27759](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as27759) (Access Haiti) fell to zero. According to a (translated) [Twitter post from the provider](https://twitter.com/AccessHaiti1/status/1570177309247938562), they had several fiber optic cables that were cut in various areas of the country, and blocked roads made it “really difficult” for their technicians to reach the problem areas. Repairs were eventually made, with traffic starting to increase again around 0830 local time (1230 UTC) on [September 15](https://twitter.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1570455180684001280), as shown in the figure below.

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns%3D%22http%3A%2F%2Fwww.w3.org%2F2000%2Fsvg%22%20viewBox%3D%220%200%201%201%22%3E%3Crect%20width%3D%221%22%20height%3D%221%22%20fill%3D%22rgb\(243%2C243%2C243\)%22%2F%3E%3C%2Fsvg%3E)![BLOG-1450 Embedded Image - owIC29](https://blog.cloudflare.com/_image?href=https%3A%2F%2Fblog.cloudflare.com%2F_emdash%2Fapi%2Fmedia%2Ffile%2F01KW45Q7ZN3WAEESN6WVG34A3S.png&w=715&h=358&f=webp&fit=cover&position=center)

Access Haiti provides [AS27774](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as27774) (Haiti Networking Group) with Internet connectivity (as an “upstream” provider), so the fiber cut impacted their connectivity as well, causing the outage shown in the figure below.

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns%3D%22http%3A%2F%2Fwww.w3.org%2F2000%2Fsvg%22%20viewBox%3D%220%200%201%201%22%3E%3Crect%20width%3D%221%22%20height%3D%221%22%20fill%3D%22rgb\(243%2C243%2C243\)%22%2F%3E%3C%2Fsvg%3E)![BLOG-1450 Embedded Image - Tck6On](https://blog.cloudflare.com/_image?href=https%3A%2F%2Fblog.cloudflare.com%2F_emdash%2Fapi%2Fmedia%2Ffile%2F01KW499QNCF0FJ2YE0F630P8XS.png&w=715&h=358&f=webp&fit=cover&position=center)

## Technical problems

As a heading, “technical problems” can be a catch-all, referring to multiple types of issues, including misconfigurations and routing problems. However, it is also sometimes the official explanation given by a government or telecommunications company for an observed Internet disruption.

### Rogers

Arguably the most significant Internet disruption so far this year took place on [AS812](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as812) (Rogers), one of Canada’s largest Internet service providers. At around 0845 UTC on July 8, a near complete loss of traffic was observed, as seen in the figure below.

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns%3D%22http%3A%2F%2Fwww.w3.org%2F2000%2Fsvg%22%20viewBox%3D%220%200%201%201%22%3E%3Crect%20width%3D%221%22%20height%3D%221%22%20fill%3D%22rgb\(243%2C243%2C243\)%22%2F%3E%3C%2Fsvg%3E)![BLOG-1450 Embedded Image - CJcB2Y](https://blog.cloudflare.com/_image?href=https%3A%2F%2Fblog.cloudflare.com%2F_emdash%2Fapi%2Fmedia%2Ffile%2F01KW482S72M56SJPGV93ASYEZB.png&w=715&h=358&f=webp&fit=cover&position=center)

The figure below shows that small amounts of traffic were seen from the network over the course of the outage, but it took nearly 24 hours for traffic to return to normal levels.

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns%3D%22http%3A%2F%2Fwww.w3.org%2F2000%2Fsvg%22%20viewBox%3D%220%200%201%201%22%3E%3Crect%20width%3D%221%22%20height%3D%221%22%20fill%3D%22rgb\(243%2C243%2C243\)%22%2F%3E%3C%2Fsvg%3E)![BLOG-1570 Embedded Image - aD9LxF](https://blog.cloudflare.com/_image?href=https%3A%2F%2Fblog.cloudflare.com%2F_emdash%2Fapi%2Fmedia%2Ffile%2F01KW46B5BWEJSPDVVKX3012ASW.png&w=715&h=358&f=webp&fit=cover&position=center)

A [notice](https://about.rogers.com/news-ideas/a-message-from-rogers-president-and-ceo/) posted by the Rogers CEO explained that “ _We now believe we’ve narrowed the cause to a network system failure following a maintenance update in our core network, which caused some of our routers to malfunction early Friday morning. We disconnected the specific equipment and redirected traffic, which allowed our network and services to come back online over time as we managed traffic volumes returning to normal levels._ ” A Cloudflare [blog post](https://blog.cloudflare.com/cloudflares-view-of-the-rogers-communications-outage-in-canada/) covered the Rogers outage in real-time, highlighting related BGP activity and small increases of traffic.

### Chad

A four-hour near-complete Internet outage took place in Chad on [August 12](https://twitter.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1558084164389404674), occurring between 1045 and 1300 local time (0945 to 1400 UTC). Authorities in Chad said that the disruption was [due to a “technical problem”](https://twitter.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1558131821438722048) on connections between [Sudachad](https://www.sudachad.com/) and networks in Cameroon and Sudan.

## Conclusion

As part of Cloudflare’s Birthday Week at the end of September, we [launched the Cloudflare Radar Outage Center (CROC)](https://blog.cloudflare.com/announcing-cloudflare-radar-outage-center/). The [CROC](https://radar.cloudflare.com/outage-center) is a section of our new [Radar 2.0](https://radar.cloudflare.com/) site that archives information about observed Internet disruptions. The underlying data that powers the CROC is also available through an [API](https://api.cloudflare.com/#radar-annotations-get-outages-annotations), enabling interested parties to incorporate data into their own tools, sites, and applications.

# Shutdown season: the Q2 2025 Internet disruption summary

Cloudflare’s [_network_](https://www.cloudflare.com/network/) currently spans more than 330 cities in over 125 countries, and we interconnect with over 13,000 network providers in order to provide a broad range of services to millions of customers. The breadth of both our network and our customer base provides us with a unique perspective on Internet resilience, enabling us to observe the impact of Internet disruptions at both a local and national level, as well as at a network level.

As we have noted in the past, this post is intended as a summary overview of observed and confirmed disruptions, and is not an exhaustive or complete list of issues that have occurred during the quarter. A larger list of detected traffic anomalies is available in the [_Cloudflare Radar Outage Center_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/outage-center#traffic-anomalies). Note that both bytes-based and request-based traffic graphs are used within the post to illustrate the impact of the observed disruptions — the choice of metric was generally made based on which better illustrated the impact of the disruption.

In our [_Q1 2025 summary post_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q1-2025-internet-disruption-summary/), we noted that we had not observed any government-directed Internet shutdowns during the quarter. Unfortunately, that forward progress was short-lived — in the second quarter of 2025, we observed _shutdowns_ in Libya, Iran, Iraq, Syria, and Panama. The Internet’s reliance on a stable electric grid was made abundantly clear during the quarter, with a massive _power outage_ impacting Spain and Portugal disrupting connectivity within those countries. Fiber optic _cable cuts_ impacted providers in Haiti and Malawi, major North American providers saw _technical problems_ disrupt Internet traffic, and a Russian provider was once again targeted by a significant _cyberattack_, knocking the network offline. Unfortunately, official attribution of an Internet outage’s root cause isn’t always available — and we observed several significant, yet _unexplained_, Internet outages during the quarter.

## Government-directed shutdowns

### Libya

On May 16, Internet disruptions were observed across multiple [_Libyan_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/ly) network providers, with connectivity reportedly shut down in response to [_public protests_](https://libyareview.com/55698/protestors-face-internet-shutdown-in-libyan-capital/) against the Government of National Unity. Starting at 13:30 UTC (15:30 local time), traffic dropped by more than 50% as compared to the prior week at [_Libyan International Company for Technology (AS329129)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as329129?dateStart=2025-05-16&dateEnd=2025-05-17#traffic-trends), [_Giga Communication (AS328539)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as328539?dateStart=2025-05-16&dateEnd=2025-05-17#traffic-trends), [_Aljeel Aljadeed for Technology (AS37284)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as37284?dateStart=2025-05-16&dateEnd=2025-05-17#traffic-trends), and [_Awal Telecom (AS328733)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as328733?dateStart=2025-05-16&dateEnd=2025-05-17#traffic-trends), with the latter experiencing a complete outage. Lower traffic volumes were observed until around 00:00 UTC (02:00 local time), with traffic restoration occurring within an hour or so on either side. Giga Communication (AS328539) experienced a second disruption on May 17 between 02:00 - 11:30 UTC (04:00 - 13:30 local time).

### Iran

Multiple Internet shutdowns occurred in [_Iran_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/ir) in June following Israel’s initial [_attacks on the country’s nuclear sites_](https://apnews.com/article/iran-explosions-israel-tehran-00234a06e5128a8aceb406b140297299). The first, on June 13, occurred between 07:15 - 09:45 UTC (10:45 - 13:15 local time). Iran’s Ministry of Communications [_issued a statement_](https://x.com/itiransite/status/1933475023244648514) that announced the shutdown: “ _In light of the country's special circumstances and based on the measures taken by the competent authorities, temporary restrictions have been imposed on the country's Internet. It is obvious that these restrictions will be lifted once normal conditions are restored._ ” This shutdown order impacted network providers including [_FanapTelecom (AS24631)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as24631?dateStart=2025-06-13&dateEnd=2025-06-13#http-traffic), Rasana ([_AS205647_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as205647?dateStart=2025-06-13&dateEnd=2025-06-13#http-traffic) and [_AS31549_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as31549?dateStart=2025-06-13&dateEnd=2025-06-13#http-traffic)), [_MCCI (AS197207)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as197207?dateStart=2025-06-13&dateEnd=2025-06-13#http-traffic), and [_TCI (AS58224)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as58224?dateStart=2025-06-13&dateEnd=2025-06-13#http-traffic), as well as others.

On June 17, Internet connectivity was again restricted, this time [_reportedly in an effort to “ward off cyber attacks”_](https://x.com/Digiato/status/1934561401185432046), according to a government spokesperson. This second round of shutdowns began at 17:30 local time (14:00 UTC), impacting multiple networks. Traffic recovered at 15:30 UTC (19:00 local time) on [_FanapTelecom (AS24631)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as24631?dateStart=2025-06-17&dateEnd=2025-06-17#http-traffic) and [_Pars Online (AS16322)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as16322?dateStart=2025-06-17&dateEnd=2025-06-17#http-traffic), at 20:00 UTC (23:30 local time) on [_MCCI (AS197207)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as197207?dateStart=2025-06-17&dateEnd=2025-06-17#http-traffic) and [_IranCell (AS44244)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as44244?dateStart=2025-06-17&dateEnd=2025-06-17#http-traffic), at 22:00 UTC on June 17 (01:30 on June 18 local time) on [_RighTel (AS57218)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as57218?dateStart=2025-06-17&dateEnd=2025-06-17#http-traffic), and at 06:00 UTC on June 18 (09:30 local time) on Rasana ([_AS31549_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as31549?dateStart=2025-06-17&dateEnd=2025-06-18#http-traffic) and [_AS205647_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as205647?dateStart=2025-06-17&dateEnd=2025-06-18#http-traffic)).

During these initial Internet shutdowns, incoming Internet traffic was [_reportedly_](https://filter.watch/english/2025/06/19/network-monitoring-june-iran-internet-status-week-1-of-israel-iran-war/) also blocked, and user access was limited to Iran’s domestic [_“National Information Network” (NIN)_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Information_Network).

Just a day later, on June 18, an extended third shutdown was put into place, this one lasting from 12:50 UTC (16:20 local time) through 05:00 UTC (08:30 local time) on June 25. Once again, the shutdown was [_reportedly implemented as a means of protecting against cyberattacks_](https://techcrunch.com/2025/06/20/irans-government-says-it-shut-down-internet-to-protect-against-cyberattacks/), with a government spokesperson commenting “ _We have previously stated that if necessary, we will certainly switch to a national internet and restrict global internet access. Security is our main concern, and we are witnessing cyberattacks on the country’s critical infrastructure and disruptions in the functioning of banks. Many of the enemy’s drones are managed and controlled via the internet, and a large amount of information is exchanged this way. A cryptocurrency exchange was also hacked, and considering all these issues, we have decided to impose Internet restrictions._ ” This shutdown resulted in a near-complete loss of traffic through 02:00 UTC (05:30 local time) on June 21, when some traffic recovery was observed, though at levels remaining well-below pre-shutdown volumes. Traffic from this partial recovery settled into a consistent cycle for several days, until returning to expected levels on June 25. The same network providers impacted by the previous shutdowns were affected by this one as well.

### Iraq

Consistent with measures taken over the past several years ([_2024_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/syria-iraq-algeria-exam-internet-shutdown/), [_2023_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/exam-internet-shutdowns-iraq-algeria/), [_2022_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q2-2022-internet-disruption-summary/#schools-in-internets-out)), governments in [_Iraq_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/iq) again implemented regular Internet shutdowns in an effort to prevent cheating on national exams. (We say “governments” here because the shutdowns took place both in the main part of the country and in the Iraqi Kurdistan region in the northern part of the country.)

Occurring between 03:00 - 05:00 UTC ([_06:00 - 08:00 local time_](https://www.moc.gov.iq/?article=1015)) at the request of the Ministry of Education, the shutdowns in the main part of the country started on May 20 and ran through June 4 for middle school exams, and from June 14 until July 3 for [_preparatory school exams_](https://www.facebook.com/Iraq.Ministry.of.Education/posts/pfbid0a7VuMttRxdoGWwuaymy38LcZw9jscz3Dfxup4aUue2LeRBPuU2c7vnDsZKbgCkE2l). Network providers that implemented the shutdowns included [_Earthlink (AS199739)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as199739), [_Asiacell (AS51684)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as51684), [_Zainas (AS59588)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as59588), [_Halasat (AS58322)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as58322), and [_HulumTele (AS203214)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as203214).

In the Kurdistan region, the shutdowns began June 1, and ran through July 6, [_taking place between 03:30 - 04:30 UTC (06:30 - 07:30 local time)_](https://x.com/TwanaOth/status/1930380416374002119) on Wednesdays and Sundays. Network providers that implemented the shutdowns included [_IQ Online (AS48492)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as48492), [_KorekTel (AS59625)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as59625), [_Newroz Telecom (AS21277)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as21277), and [_KNET (AS206206)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as206206).

### Syria

As Iraq does, [_Syria_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/sy) also implements nationwide Internet shutdowns to prevent cheating on exams, and has been doing so for several years ([_2021_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/syria-exam-related-internet-shutdowns/), [_2022_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/syria-sudan-algeria-exam-internet-shutdown/), [_2023_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q2-2023-internet-disruption-summary/#syria), [_2024_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/syria-iraq-algeria-exam-internet-shutdown/)). However, in contrast to previous years, in 2025, the government only ordered the cutoff of cellular connectivity, with a [_published statement_](https://t.me/TrbyaGov/1869) noting (translated) “ _As part of our commitment to ensuring the integrity of public examinations and safeguarding the future of our dear students, and based on our national responsibility to secure a fair and transparent examination environment,**a temporary cellular communications blackout will be implemented in areas near examination centers across the Syrian Arab Republic**. … The cellular communications blackout will be implemented exclusively within the narrowest possible geographical and timeframe, during the time students are in exam halls._”

During the second quarter, the shutdowns associated with the “Basic Education Certificate” took place on June 21, 24, and 29 between 05:15 - 06:00 UTC (08:15 - 09:00 local time). Exams and associated shutdowns for the “Secondary Education Certificate” are scheduled to take place between July 12 and August 3.

Because these shutdowns only impacted mobile connectivity, they only resulted in a partial drop in announced IP address space, as opposed to a more complete loss [_as seen in previous years_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/sy?dateStart=2024-05-19&dateEnd=2024-06-15#announced-ip-address-space).

### Panama

On June 21, an [_X post_](https://x.com/AsepPanama/status/1936462415278854469) from [_ASEP Panamá_](https://asep.gob.pa/) (the telecommunications regulating agency) announced that (translated) “ _...in compliance with Cabinet Decree No. 27 of June 20, 2025, and by formal instruction from the Ministry of Government, the temporary suspension of mobile telephony and residential internet services in the province of Bocas del Toro has been coordinated._ ” The suspension, according to the post, was supposed to be in place until June 25, however a [_subsequent X post_](https://x.com/AsepPanama/status/1937982698624057637) noted that it would be extended until Sunday, June 29, 2025.

The suspension of Internet connectivity was [_implemented in response to_](https://www.ipandetec.org/panama/panama-debe-restablecer-internet-bocas/) protests and demonstrations against reforms to the Social Security Fund, retirement, and pensions, specifically in the province of Bocas del Toro.

The graph below shows an effective loss of traffic from [_Cable Onda (AS18809)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as18809) in Bocas Del Toro, [_Panama_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/pa) around 03:30 UTC on June 21 (22:30 local time on June 20), recovering around 06:00 UTC (01:00 local time) on June 30. The recovery is in line with the [_final related X post_](https://x.com/AsepPanama/status/1939682983440421070) from ASEP, which noted (translated) “ _... Internet and cellular telephone services in the province of Bocas del Toro have been restored as of 12:01 a.m. on Monday, June 30…_ ”.

## Power outages lead to Internet outages

### Portugal & Spain

The big power outage story during the second quarter was the massive outage across much of [_Portugal_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/pt) and [_Spain_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/es) on April 28. The impact of the event was covered in detail in the [__How the April 28, 2025, power outage in Portugal and Spain impacted Internet traffic and connectivity_ blog post_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/how-power-outage-in-portugal-spain-impacted-internet/), which explored shifts in traffic at a country/network/regional level, as well as how the power outage impacted network quality and announced IP address space.

In Portugal, Internet traffic dropped as the power grid failed — when compared with the previous week, traffic fell ~50 % immediately and within five hours it was ~90% below the week before.

In Spain, Internet traffic dropped as the power grid failed, with traffic immediately dropping by around 60% as compared to the previous week, falling to approximately 80% below the previous week within the next five hours.

In both countries, traffic returned to expected levels around 01:00 local time (midnight UTC) on April 29. More details about the outage can be found in the blog post linked above.

### Morocco

It appears that [_Morocco_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/ma) may have also been impacted in some fashion by the Portugal/Spain power outage, or at least Orange Maroc was. In a [_post on X_](https://x.com/OrangeMaroc/status/1916866583047147690), the provider stated (translated) “ _Internet traffic has been disrupted following a massive power outage in Spain and Portugal, which is affecting international connections._ ” [_Traffic from the network (AS36925)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as36925?dateStart=2025-04-28&dateEnd=2025-04-29) fell sharply around 12:00 UTC (13:00 local time), 90 minutes after the power outage began, with a full outage beginning around 15:00 UTC (16:00 local time). Traffic returned to expected levels around 23:30 UTC on April 28 (00:30 local time on April 29).

### Puerto Rico

[ _Genera PR_](https://genera-pr.com/sobre-nosotros), a power company in [_Puerto Rico_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/pr), [_posted on X_](https://x.com/Genera_PR/status/1912562399741100112) on April 16 that they had (translated) _“...experienced a massive power outage across the island due to the unexpected shutdown of all generating plants, including those of Genera PR and other private generators. This situation has caused a significant disruption to electrical service…_ ” [_Luma Energy_](https://lumapr.com/), the private power company that is responsible for power distribution and power transmission in Puerto Rico, [_published their own X post_](https://x.com/lumaenergypr/status/1912554580400812243) that stated (translated) “ _Approximately at 12:40pm, an event was recorded that affects the service island-wide._ ”

Although the reported power outage was “massive” and “island-wide”, it did not have an outsized impact on [Puerto Rico’s Internet traffic](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/pr?dateStart=2025-04-13&dateEnd=2025-04-19#traffic-trends), which [_initially dropped by about 40%_](https://bsky.app/profile/radar.cloudflare.com/post/3lmxq2gfxtg2c). Over the next several days, both companies published multiple updates to their X accounts detailing the progress being made in restoring service. By 15:00 UTC (11:00 local time) on April 18, traffic had returned to expected levels, in line with a post from Luma Energy that noted (translated) “ _As of 10:00 a.m. on April 18, and thanks to LUMA’s extraordinary response and the tireless efforts of the island’s workforce—in coordination with the Puerto Rico government and generating companies—LUMA has restored electric service to 1,450,367 customers, representing 98.8% of total customers, in less than 38 hours since the island-wide outage began._ ”

As seen in the graphs below, the power outage not only impacted end-user connectivity, driving the observed drop in traffic, but also had some impact on local Internet infrastructure, with some disturbance visible to [announced IP address space](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/pr?dateStart=2025-04-13&dateEnd=2025-04-19#announced-ip-address-space).

### Saint Kitts and Nevis

A [_Facebook post_](https://www.facebook.com/skelecltd/posts/pfbid09PDXSuw7U9X3V83rvUSz7kLGnL77bqwstYgKXbkRZQQPeGDCw2pffiP1nRkRsEAxl) from [_SKELEC (The St. Kitts Electricity Company)_](https://www.skelec.kn/) on May 9 alerted customers on [_St. Kitts and Nevis_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/kn?dateStart=2025-05-09&dateEnd=2025-05-09#traffic-trends) that “ _...a fault developed at our Needsmust Power Plant resulting in an island wide outage. Restoration has begun, and complete restoration will be in two hours._ ” The post was published at 17:31 UTC (13:31 local time), approximately 30 minutes after the island’s Internet traffic initially dropped. Traffic recovery initially began around 17:45 UTC (13:45 local time), well within the two-hour estimate for complete power restoration. However, Internet traffic did not fully return to expected levels until 20:15 UTC (16:15 local time).

### North Macedonia

On May 18, it was [_reported_](https://seenews.com/news/voltage-spike-causes-power-outage-in-north-macedonia-1275427) that “ _High voltages in the regional 400 kV network amid low consumption caused a short-term outage in[ _North Macedonia_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/mk?dateStart=2025-05-18&dateEnd=2025-05-18#traffic-trends)'s 110 kV transmission network…_”, according to state-owned power company [_MEPSO_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MEPSO). While the outage reportedly impacted most of the country, MEPSO also noted that the country’s power supply was normalized within an hour after the outage began. Although brief, the power outage caused the country’s Internet traffic to drop by nearly 60% as compared to the previous week during the disruption, which occurred between 03:00 - 04:45 UTC (05:00 - 06:45 local time).

### Maldives

On June 1, Internet traffic in the [_Maldives_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/mv?dateStart=2025-06-01&dateEnd=2025-06-01#traffic-trends) dropped by nearly half as compared to the previous week when a [_widespread power outage_](https://mvrepublic.com/news/widespread-power-outage-causes-disruptions-across-greater-male/) affected the Greater Malé region. Local Internet service providers including [_Ooredoo_](https://x.com/OoredooMaldives/status/1929108987187970176) and [_Dhiraagu_](https://x.com/Dhiraagu/status/1929095794659103186) took to social media to warn subscribers of potential interruptions to both fixed and mobile broadband connections. At a country level, Internet traffic was disrupted between 07:30 - 13:00 UTC (12:30 - 18:00 local time).

The power outage also had a nominal impact on Internet infrastructure, as [announced IPv4 address space](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/mv?dateStart=2025-06-01&dateEnd=2025-06-01#announced-ip-address-space) saw a nominal drop (from 355 to 350 /24s) that began shortly after the initial drop in traffic was observed, but returned to normal as the disruption ended.

### Curaçao

A near-complete Internet outage at provider [_Flow Curaçao (AS52233)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as52233?dateStart=2025-06-14&dateEnd=2025-06-15) on June 14-15 [_sparked outrage_](https://www.curacaochronicle.com/post/opinion/flows-internet-outage-sparks-outrage-and-urgent-call-for-infrastructure-reform/) and [_demands for answers_](https://www.curacaochronicle.com/post/local/curacaos-telecom-regulator-demands-answers-from-flow-after-major-internet-outage/) by the country’s telecommunications regulator. Flow’s Internet traffic dropped significantly at 18:00 UTC (14:00 local time) on June 14, falling further in the following hours. Signs of recovery became visible around 11:00 UTC (07:00 local time) on June 15, with more complete recovery occurring at 14:00 UTC (10:00 local time). A [_Facebook post from Flow Barbados_](https://www.facebook.com/FlowBarbados/posts/pfbid02iGV1LYdNajMprF8Anvgh3KzMZbc2k9BVbVdN4C8mrVZDdcoUEhiib23TQYgisrAxl), posted on June 18, referenced a local disruption that began on June 14, but pointed at a commercial power outage at one of their key regional network facilities in Curaçao, which was likely the driver of this Internet outage.

## Fiber optic cable damage

### Digicel Haiti

Two instances of damage to its fiber optic infrastructure caused a complete Internet outage at [_Digicel Haiti (AS27653)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as27653?dateStart=2025-05-28&dateEnd=2025-05-29#http-traffic) as of 21:00 UTC (17:00 local time) on May 28, according to a (translated) [_X post_](https://x.com/jpbrun30/status/1927845676408258762) from the company’s Director General. The cable damage took the network completely off the Internet, as [announced IPv4 and IPv6 address space](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as27653?dateStart=2025-05-28&dateEnd=2025-05-29#announced-ip-address-space) also dropped to zero. Digicel Haiti remained offline until 00:45 on May 29 (20:45 local time on May 28), when both traffic and announced IP address space returned to expected levels.

### Airtel Malawi

[ _Airtel Malawi (AS37440)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as37440?dateStart=2025-06-24&dateEnd=2025-06-24#traffic-trends) experienced a 90-minute Internet outage on June 24, [_caused by ongoing vandalism on their fiber network_](https://x.com/AirtelMalawiPlc/status/1937591557684916436). Although traffic effectively disappeared between 12:30 - 14:00 UTC (14:30 - 16:00 local time), the network remained at least partially online as at least some of the network’s [IPv4 address space](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as37440?dateStart=2025-06-24&dateEnd=2025-06-24#announced-ip-address-space) continued to be announced to the Internet. Announced IPv6 address space, however, fell to zero during the duration of the outage.

## Technical problems

### Bell Canada

A [_router update_](https://x.com/Bell_Support/status/1925225503507591222) gone awry disrupted Internet service for [_Bell Canada (AS577)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as577?dateStart=2025-05-21&dateEnd=2025-05-21#http-traffic) customers in Ontario and Quebec on May 21. An [_initial X post from the provider_](https://x.com/Bell_Support/status/1925187984543883486), posted at 13:52 UTC (09:52 local time), alerted customers to the service interruption. The post trailed the start of the disruption by approximately a half hour, as traffic dropped around 13:15 UTC (09:15 local time), falling by as much as 70% as compared to the same time a week prior. Request traffic to Cloudflare’s [_1.1.1.1 DNS Resolver_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/dns/as577?dateStart=2025-05-21&dateEnd=2025-05-21#dns-query-volume) also saw a significant drop. A negligible decline in [_announced IPv4 address space_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as577?dateStart=2025-05-21&dateEnd=2025-05-21#announced-ip-address-space) was also observed.

The disruption was fairly short-lived, with traffic returning to expected levels just an hour later. A subsequent [_X post_](https://x.com/Bell_Support/status/1925225503507591222) confirmed that services had been fully restored by 15:00 UTC (11:00 local time), with [_another post_](https://x.com/Bell_Support/status/1925225526198776050) noting that the initial update had been rolled back quickly to restore service. 

### Lumen/CenturyLink 

Across parts of the [_United States_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/us), [_Lumen/CenturyLink (AS209)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as209?dateStart=2025-06-19&dateEnd=2025-06-20#traffic-trends) customers experienced a widespread Internet service disruption on June 19. Traffic volumes dropped by over 50% as compared to the prior week starting around 21:45 UTC. The disruption only lasted a couple of hours, with traffic returning to normal by 00:00 UTC on June 20.

Social media posts from affected subscribers suggested that the problem might have been DNS related, as those that switched their DNS resolver to Cloudflare’s [_1.1.1.1_](https://1.1.1.1/dns) were once again able to access the Internet. The graph below shows that [traffic to 1.1.1.1 from Lumen/CenturyLink](https://radar.cloudflare.com/explorer?dataSet=dns&loc=as209&dt=2025-06-19_2025-06-20&timeCompare=2025-06-12#result) exceeded levels seen the previous week as the disruption began, and remained elevated through June 20. Problems with an Internet service provider’s DNS resolver can appear to subscribers like an Internet outage, as they become unable to access anything requiring a DNS lookup (effectively, all Internet resources), ultimately resulting in a drop in traffic to those resources (from the affected user base), as seen in the graph above.

## Cyberattack impact

### ASVT (Russia)

[_Russian_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/ru) Internet provider [_ASVT (AS8752)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as8752?dateStart=2025-05-28&dateEnd=2025-06-05#traffic-trends) was [_reportedly_](https://therecord.media/moscow-internet-provider-asvt-ddos-attack) targeted by a major DDoS attack that resulted in a multi-day complete Internet outage. This attack followed one [_targeting Russian provider Nodex_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q1-2025-internet-disruption-summary/#russia) ([_AS29329_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/AS29329)) in March, which also caused a complete service outage. [_Reaching_](https://tadviser.com/index.php/Company:ASVT) 70.07 Gbps/6.92 million packets/second, the attack caused traffic to drop to near zero around 05:00 UTC on May 28 (08:00 Moscow time), with the effective outage lasting for approximately 10 hours. Although traffic began to return around 15:00 UTC (18:00 Moscow time), it remained below expected levels throughout the following week.

Interestingly, [query volume to Cloudflare’s 1.1.1.1 DNS Resolver from ASVT](https://radar.cloudflare.com/dns/as8752?dateStart=2025-05-28&dateEnd=2025-06-05#dns-query-volume) saw a rapid increase as traffic began to return after the initial outage, and remained elevated throughout the duration of the disruption. It isn’t clear whether the increase could be related to problems with ASVT’s native DNS resolver during the attack, forcing users to seek alternative resolvers, or if it could be related to ASVT subscribers seeking ways around damage from the attack.

## Unexplained disruptions

### Telia Finland (April 1)

According to a (now unavailable) [_“Disturbance bulletin”_](https://www.telia.fi/asiakastuki/hairiotiedote?id=sabre_858055150&lang=fi) and an [_associated X post_](https://x.com/teliafinland/status/1906966248790868230) from [_Telia Finland (AS1759)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as1759?dateStart=2025-04-01&dateEnd=2025-04-01#traffic-trends), the company acknowledged that “ _A widespread disruption has been detected in the operation of mobile network data connections and fixed broadband._ ” The widespread disruption resulted in a brief near-complete outage for subscribers between 06:30 - 07:15 UTC (09:30 - 10:15 local time).

Telia Finland did not disclose the cause of the disruption, but it is clear that it impacted IPv4 connectivity, as seen in the graph below showing [announced IPv4 address space](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as1759?dateStart=2025-04-01&dateEnd=2025-04-01#announced-ip-address-space). ([_Announced IPv6 address space_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as1759?dateStart=2025-04-01&dateEnd=2025-04-01#announced-ip-address-space) did not see any change.) This loss of IPv4 connectivity resulted in a concurrent spike in the share of traffic from Telia Finland over IPv6 — normally below 5%, it spiked above 30% during the disruption. Request traffic [_to Cloudflare’s 1.1.1.1 resolver from Telia Finland_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/dns/as1759?dateStart=2025-04-01&dateEnd=2025-04-01#dns-query-volume) also spiked at that time.

### SkyCable

Around 19:15 UTC on May 7 (03:15 local time on May 8), subscribers of [_SkyCable (AS23944)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as23944?dateStart=2025-05-07&dateEnd=2025-05-08#traffic-trends) in the [_Philippines_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/ph) experienced a complete Internet outage. Internet traffic from the network dropped to zero, as did [announced IPv4 address space](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as23944?dateStart=2025-05-07&dateEnd=2025-05-08#announced-ip-address-space). The disruption lasted until 03:00 UTC on May 8 (11:00 local time), and SkyCable did not publish any information regarding the cause of the eight-hour service outage.

### TrueMove H

On May 22, [_Thai_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/th) mobile provider [_TrueMove H (AS132061)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as132061?dateStart=2025-05-22&dateEnd=2025-05-22#http-traffic) [_suffered a nationwide outage_](https://www.kaohooninternational.com/markets/558192), impacting connectivity for subscribers. The provider [_acknowledged and apologized_](https://www.nationthailand.com/news/general/40050305) for the disruption, but did not provide an official reason for the outage. (An [_article_](https://www.nationthailand.com/news/general/40050309) in the local press reported “ _that the outage was caused by technical errors on True’s computer servers_ ” and also stated that others suggested that “ _the problem might have been caused by an error on True’s DNS servers_ ”.)

At 03:00 UTC (10:00 local time), traffic initially dropped by over 80% as compared to the prior week. Almost immediately, traffic began to slowly recover, and returned to expected levels around 08:00 UTC (15:00 local time). A brief [_partial drop in announced IPv4 address space was also observed_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as132061?dateStart=2025-05-22&dateEnd=2025-05-22#announced-ip-address-space) during the first hour of the disruption.

### Digicel Haiti

Two days after experiencing _an outage due to cable damage_, [_Digicel Haiti (AS27653)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as27653?dateStart=2025-05-30&dateEnd=2025-05-30#traffic-trends) experienced another complete outage on May 30. In contrast to the previous outage, no additional information about this one was published on social media by [_Digicel Haiti_](https://x.com/DigicelHT) or its [_Director General_](https://x.com/jpbrun30). The network effectively disappeared from the Internet at 14:15 UTC (10:15 local time), with both traffic and [announced IP address space](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as27653?dateStart=2025-05-30&dateEnd=2025-05-30#announced-ip-address-space) (IPv4 & IPv6) dropping to zero. The outage lasted nearly three hours, with traffic and announced IP space all returning around 17:00 UTC (13:00 local time).

### Syria

On June 10, an Internet outage in [_Syria_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/sy?dateStart=2025-06-10&dateEnd=2025-06-10#traffic-trends) [_reportedly_](https://www.profilenews.com/en/breaking-internet-outage-in-syria/) affected the ADSL landline network across multiple provinces. Traffic dropped by as much as two-thirds below the same time the previous week at 08:15 UTC (11:15 local time), with the disruption lasting two hours. [Announced IPv4 address space](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/sy?dateStart=2025-06-10&dateEnd=2025-06-10#announced-ip-address-space) also fell during the course of the outage, indicating a potential infrastructure issue. However, as seen below, [request volume from Syria to Cloudflare’s 1.1.1.1 DNS resolver](https://radar.cloudflare.com/dns/sy?dateStart=2025-06-10&dateEnd=2025-06-10#dns-query-volume) was also elevated during the outage. This behavior has been observed in the past during government-directed shutdowns of Internet connectivity in Syria, when traffic can leave the country, but not return. There was no other indication that this outage was due to an intentional shutdown, but no official explanation for the disruption was available.

## Conclusion

Government-directed Internet shutdowns returned with a vengeance in the second quarter, and that trend continues into the third quarter, though the latest ones have been exam-related, and not driven by protests. And while power-outage related Internet disruptions have frequently been observed in the past, often in smaller countries with less stable infrastructure, the massive outage in Spain and Portugal on April 28 reminds us that much like the Internet, electrical infrastructure is often interconnected across countries, meaning that problems in one can potentially cause significant problems in others.

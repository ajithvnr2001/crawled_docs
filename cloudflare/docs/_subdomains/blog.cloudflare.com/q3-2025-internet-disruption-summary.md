In the third quarter, we observed Internet disruptions with a wide variety of known causes, as well as several with _no definitive or published cause_. Once again, we unfortunately saw a number of _government-directed shutdowns_, including exam-related shutdowns in _Sudan_, _Syria_, and _Iraq_. _Cable cuts_, both submarine and terrestrial, caused Internet outages, including one caused by a _stray bullet_. _A rogue contractor_, among other events, caused power outages that impacted Internet connectivity. Damage from an _earthquake_ and a _fire_ caused service disruptions, as did a targeted _cyberattack_. And a myriad of _technical issues_, including issues with _China’s Great Firewall_, resulted in traffic losses across multiple countries.

As we have noted in the past, this post is intended as a summary overview of observed and confirmed disruptions, and is not an exhaustive or complete list of issues that have occurred during the quarter. A larger list of detected traffic anomalies is available in the [_Cloudflare Radar Outage Center_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/outage-center#traffic-anomalies). These anomalies are detected through significant deviations from expected traffic patterns observed across our network. Note that both bytes-based and request-based traffic graphs are used within the post to illustrate the impact of the observed disruptions — the choice of metric to include was generally made based on which better illustrated the impact of the disruption.

## Government-directed shutdowns

### Sudan

Regular drops in traffic from [_Sudan_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/sd) were observed between 12:00-15:00 UTC (14:00-17:00 local time) each day from July 7-10. Partial outages were observed at [_Sudatel (AS15706)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as15706?dateStart=2025-07-06&dateEnd=2025-07-12#http-traffic), and near-complete outages at [_SDN Mobitel (AS36998)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as36998?dateStart=2025-07-06&dateEnd=2025-07-12#http-traffic) and [_MTN Sudan (AS36972)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as36972?dateStart=2025-07-06&dateEnd=2025-07-12#http-traffic). Similar drops were also seen in traffic to our [_1.1.1.1 DNS resolver_](https://1.1.1.1/dns) from these impacted [_ASNs_](https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/network-layer/what-is-an-autonomous-system/).

We have observed Sudan implementing government-directed Internet shutdowns in the past ([_2021_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/sudans-exam-related-internet-shutdowns/), [_2022_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/syria-sudan-algeria-exam-internet-shutdown/#sudan)), and given that the timing aligns with the last four days of [_postponed 2024 secondary school certificate examinations_](https://www.suna-sd.net/posts/ministry-of-education-publishes-schedule-for-postponed-2024-secondary-school-certificate-examinations), in addition to fitting the pattern of short-duration disruptions repeating across multiple days, we believe that these drops in traffic were exam-related shutdowns as well. 

### Syria

In our [_second quarter post_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q2-2025-internet-disruption-summary/#syria), we covered the cellular connectivity-focused exam-related Internet shutdowns that [_Syria_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/sy) chose to implement this year in an effort to limit their impact. During the second quarter, the shutdowns associated with the “Basic Education Certificate” took place on June 21, 24, and 29 between 05:15 - 06:00 UTC (08:15 - 09:00 local time). Exams and associated shutdowns for the “Secondary Education Certificate” were scheduled to take place between July 12 and August 3, and during that period, we observed six additional Internet disruptions in Syria on July 12, 17, 21, 28, 31, and August 3, as shown in the graph below.

At the end of the exam period, the [_Syrian Ministry of Education posted a Telegram message_](https://t.me/TrbyaGov/2352) that was presumably intended to justify the shutdowns, and the focus on cellular connectivity. Translated, it said in part:

“ _As part of its efforts to ensure the integrity of the examination process, and in coordination with relevant authorities, the Ministry of Education was able to uncover organized exam cheating networks in three examination centers in Lattakia Governorate. These networks used advanced electronic technologies and devices in their attempt to manipulate the exam process._

_The network was seized in cooperation with the Lattakia Education Directorate, following close monitoring and detection of suspicious attempts. It was found that members of the network used small earphones, wireless communication devices, and mobile phones equipped with advanced transmission and reception technologies, which contradict educational values and violate the integrity of the examination process and the principle of justice._ ”

### Venezuela 

A slightly more unusual government directed shutdown took place in [_Venezuela_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/ve) on August 18 when Venezuelan provider [_SuperCable (AS22313)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as22313) ceased service. An [_X post_](https://x.com/vesinfiltro/status/1957601745321783746) from Venezuelan industry watcher [_VE sin Filtro_](https://vesinfiltro.org/) published a notification from [_CONATEL, the National Commission of Telecommunications in Venezuela_](https://conatel.gob.ve/), that notified SuperCable that as of March 14, 2025, its authority to operate in the country had been revoked, and established a 60 day transition period so that users could find another provider. Another [_X post from VE sin Filtro_](https://x.com/vesinfiltro/status/1957595268221632929) shared an email that SuperCable subscribers received from the company announcing the end of the service and, and noted that half an hour after the email was sent, subscribers were left without Internet connectivity. Traffic began to fall at 15:00 UTC (11:00 local time), and was gone after 15:30 UTC (11:30 local time). Connectivity remained shut down through the end of the quarter.

Interestingly, we did not see a corresponding full loss of announced IP address space when traffic disappeared. However, such full losses did occur between [_August 19-21_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as22313?dateStart=2025-08-17&dateEnd=2025-08-23), and again briefly on [_September 16_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as22313?dateStart=2025-09-14&dateEnd=2025-09-20). The number of announced /24s (blocks of 256 IPv4 addresses) fell from 95 to 63 on [_September 25_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as22313?dateStart=2025-09-24&dateEnd=2025-09-30), and remained at that level through the end of the quarter.

### Iraq

Similar to Syria, we covered the latest rounds of exam-related Internet shutdowns in [_Iraq_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/iq) in our [_second quarter blog post_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q2-2025-internet-disruption-summary/#iraq). In that post, we noted that the shutdowns in the main part of the country ran until July 3 for [_preparatory school exams_](https://www.facebook.com/Iraq.Ministry.of.Education/posts/pfbid0a7VuMttRxdoGWwuaymy38LcZw9jscz3Dfxup4aUue2LeRBPuU2c7vnDsZKbgCkE2l), and through July 6 in the Kurdistan region. These can be seen in the graph below.

The [_Kurdistan Regional Government in Iraq ordered Internet services to be suspended_](https://pulse.internetsociety.org/en/shutdowns/exams-shutdown-kurdistan-iraq-25-august-2025/) on August 23 between 03:30 and 04:45 UTC (6:30-7:45 local time), and again every Saturday, Monday, and Wednesday until September 8 to prevent cheating on the [_second round of grade 12 exams_](https://www.kurdistan24.net/ckb/story/859388/%D9%88%DB%95%D8%B2%D8%A7%D8%B1%DB%95%D8%AA%DB%8C-%DA%AF%D9%88%D8%A7%D8%B3%D8%AA%D9%86%DB%95%D9%88%DB%95-%D9%84%DB%95-%DA%95%DB%86%DA%98%D8%A7%D9%86%DB%8C-%D8%AA%D8%A7%D9%82%DB%8C%DA%A9%D8%B1%D8%AF%D9%86%DB%95%D9%88%DB%95%DA%A9%D8%A7%D9%86%DB%8C-%D9%BE%DB%86%D9%84%DB%8C-12-%D9%87%DB%8E%DA%B5%DB%95%DA%A9%D8%A7%D9%86%DB%8C-%D8%A6%DB%8C%D9%86%D8%AA%DB%95%D8%B1%D9%86%DB%8E%D8%AA-%DA%95%D8%A7%D8%AF%DB%95%DA%AF%DB%8C%D8%B1%DB%8E%D9%86). Similar to last quarter, [_KNET (AS206206)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as206206), [_Newroz Telecom (AS21277)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as21277), [_IQ Online (AS48492)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as48492), and [_KorekTel (AS59625)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as59625) were impacted by the ordered shutdowns.

In the main part of the country, starting on August 26, the latest round of [_Internet shutdowns for high school exams_](https://pulse.internetsociety.org/en/shutdowns/internet-shutdown-for-iraq-exam-26-august-2025/) began, scheduled through September 13, taking place between 03:00-05:00 UTC (06:00-08:00 local time). Networks impacted by these shutdowns included [_Earthlink (AS199739)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as199739), [_Asiacell (AS51684)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as51684), [_Zainas (AS59588)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as59588), [_Halasat (AS58322)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as58322), and [_HulumTele (AS203214)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as203214).

### Afghanistan

In mid-September, the Taliban [_ordered the shutdown of fiber optic Internet connectivity_](https://amu.tv/200798/) in multiple provinces across [_Afghanistan_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/af), as part of a drive to “prevent immorality”. It was the first such ban issued since the Taliban took full control of the country in August 2021. As many as [_15 provinces_](https://amu.tv/200798/) experienced shutdowns, and these regional shutdowns [_blocked_](https://www.afghanstudiescenter.org/taliban-internet-shutdown-blocks-thousands-of-afghan-students-from-online-classes/) Afghani students from attending online classes, [_impacted_](https://theweek.com/world-news/afghanistan-taliban-high-speed-internet-women-education) commerce and banking, and [_limited access_](https://www.dw.com/en/afghanistan-whats-at-stake-as-taliban-cut-internet/a-74043564) to government agencies and institutions such as passport and registration offices, customs offices.

Less than two weeks later, just after 11:30 UTC (16:00 local time) on Monday, September 29, 2025, subscribers of wired Internet providers in [_Afghanistan_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/af) experienced a [_brief service interruption_](https://x.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1972649804821057727), lasting until just before 12:00 UTC (16:30 local time). Mobile providers [_Afghan Wireless (AS38472) and Etisalat (AS131284)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/explorer?dataSet=netflows&loc=&dt=1d&asn=as131284&compAsn=as38742&timeCompare=2025-09-21) remained available during that period. However, just after 12:30 UTC (17:00 local time), the Internet was [_completely shut down_](https://x.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1972682041759076637), taking the country completely offline.

These shutdowns are reviewed in more detail in our September 30 blog post, [__Nationwide Internet shutdown in Afghanistan extends localized disruptions__](https://blog.cloudflare.com/nationwide-internet-shutdown-in-afghanistan/). Connectivity was restored around 11:45 UTC (16:15 local time) on October 1.

## Fiber optic cable damage

### Dominican Republic

On July 7, a [_post on X from Claro_](https://x.com/ClaroRD/status/1942286349006168091) alerted subscribers to a service disruption caused by damage to two fiber optic cables. According to a [_subsequent post_](https://x.com/ClaroRD/status/1942368212160516305), one was damaged by work being done by [_CORAAVEGA_](http://coraavega.gob.do) (La Vega Water And Sewerage Corporation) and the other by work being done by the Dominican Electric Transmission Company. As a result of the damage, traffic from [_Claro (AS6400)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as6400) began to drop just before 16:00 UTC (12:00 local time), falling just over two-thirds compared to the prior week. Claro’s technicians were able to quickly locate the faults and repair them, with traffic recovering around 18:00 UTC (14:00 local time).

### Angola

Between 12:45-15:45 UTC (13:45-16:45 local time) on July 19, users in [_Angola_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/ao) experienced an Internet disruption, with [_Unitel Angola (AS37119)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as37119) experiencing [_as much as a 95% drop in traffic_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/explorer?dataSet=netflows&loc=as37119&dt=2025-07-19_2025-07-19&timeCompare=2025-07-12#query) as compared to the previous week, and [_Connectis (AS327932)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/as327932?dateStart=2025-07-19&dateEnd=2025-07-19) suffering a complete outage. According to an [_X post from Unitel Angola_](https://x.com/unitelao/status/1946644209370358120), it “ _was caused by a disruption at our partner Angola Cables, resulting from public road works that affected the national fiber optic interconnections._ ”

However, the timing of the disruption coincided with protests over the rise in diesel fuel prices, and local non-governmental organizations [_disputed_](https://www.verangola.net/va/en/072025/Society/45242/Angolan-NGOs-consider-internet-shutdown-during-Saturday%27s-protests-a-dictatorial-measure.htm) Unitel Angola’s explanation, [_claiming_](https://myemail.constantcontact.com/STATEMENT-OF-REPUDIATION--ON-THE-INTERNET-SHUTDOWN-DURING-THE-DEMONSTRATIONS-OF-JULY-19-.html) that it was actually due to a government-directed Internet shutdown. Multiple Angolan network providers experienced a drop in announced IP address space during the period the Internet disruption occurred, and analysis of routing information for these networks finds that they share [_Angola Cables (AS37468)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as37468) as an upstream provider, lending some credence to the explanation from Unitel Angola.

### Haiti

[ _Digicel Haiti (AS27653)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as27653) is no stranger to Internet disruptions caused by damage to both terrestrial and submarine cables, experiencing such problems during the [_first_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q1-2025-internet-disruption-summary/#haiti) and [_second_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q2-2025-internet-disruption-summary/#digicel-haiti) quarters of 2025, as well as [_first_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q1-2024-internet-disruption-summary/#digicel-haiti), [_second_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q2-2024-internet-disruption-summary/#haiti), and [_third_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q3-2024-internet-disruption-summary/#haiti) quarters of 2024. The most recent such disruption occurred on August 26, when they experienced two different cuts on their fiber optic infrastructure, [_according to an X post_](https://x.com/jpbrun30/status/1960437559558869220) from the company’s Director General. Traffic [_dropped by approximately 80%_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/explorer?dataSet=http&loc=as27653&dt=2025-08-26_2025-08-26&timeCompare=2025-08-19#result) during the disruption, which lasted from 19:30-23:00 UTC (15:30-19:00 UTC).

### Pakistan & United Arab Emirates

Telegeography’s [_Submarine Cable Map_](https://www.submarinecablemap.com/) shows that the Red Sea has a high density of submarine cables that carry data between Europe, Africa, and Asia. Cuts to these cables [_can significantly impact connectivity_](https://www.wired.com/story/houthi-internet-cables-ship-anchor-path/), ranging from increased latency on international connections to complete outages. The impacts may only affect a single country, or they may disrupt multiple countries connected to a damaged cable. On September 6, [_Pakistan Telecom (AS17557)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as17557) [_posted a message on X_](https://x.com/PTCLOfficial/status/1964203180876521559) that stated “ _We would like to inform that submarine cable cuts have occurred in Saudi waters near Jeddah, impacting partial bandwidth capacity on[ _SMW4_](https://www.submarinecablemap.com/submarine-cable/seamewe-4) and [_IMEWE_](https://www.submarinecablemap.com/submarine-cable/imewe) systems. As a result, internet users in Pakistan may experience some service degradation during peak hours._” (Initial reporting that the cable cuts occurred near Jeddah were apparently incorrect, as the [_damage occurred in Yemeni waters_](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7379509758598406144?commentUrn=urn%3Ali%3Acomment%3A%28activity%3A7379509758598406144%2C7379684775701245952%29&dashCommentUrn=urn%3Ali%3Afsd_comment%3A%287379684775701245952%2Curn%3Ali%3Aactivity%3A7379509758598406144%29).)

In the [_United Arab Emirates_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/ae), Etisalat alerted customers via [_a post on X_](https://x.com/eAndUAE/status/1964655864117346578) that they “ _may experience slowness in data services due to an interruption in the international submarine cables._ ” Between 11:00-22:00 UTC (15:00-02:00 local time) on September 6, traffic from [_AS8966 (Etisalat)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as8966) [_dropped as much as 28%_](https://x.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1964727360764469339).

Also in the UAE, service provider [_du (AS15802)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as15802) told their customers via a post on X that “ _You may experience some slowness in our data services due to an International submarine cable cut._ ” This slowness is visible in Radar’s Internet quality metrics for the network between 11:00-22:00 UTC (15:00-02:00 local time) on September 6, with [_median bandwidth_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/quality/as15802?dateStart=2025-09-06&dateEnd=2025-09-06#bandwidth) dropping by more than half, from 25 Mbps to as low as 9.8 Mbps, and [_median latency_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/quality/as15802?dateStart=2025-09-06&dateEnd=2025-09-06#latency) doubling from 30 ms to over 60 ms.

### Czech Republic

According to the Industry and Trade Ministry in the [_Czech Republic_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/cz), [_a fallen power cable caused a widespread power outage_](https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/czech-republic-hit-by-major-power-outage-2025-07-04/) on July 4. ### Yemen

## Technical problems

### Starlink

Global satellite Internet service provider [_Starlink (AS14593)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as14593) acknowledged a July 24 network outage through a [_post on X_](https://x.com/Starlink/status/1948474586699571518). The Vice President of Network Engineering at SpaceX explained, in a [_subsequent X post_](https://x.com/michaelnicollsx/status/1948509258024452488), that “ _The outage was due to failure of key internal software services that operate the core network._ ”

### China

At around 16:30 UTC on August 19 (00:30 local time on August 20), we observed an anomalous 25% drop in [_China’s_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/cn) Internet traffic. Our analysis of related metrics found that this disruption caused a drop in the share of IPv4 traffic, as well as a spike in the share of HTTP traffic (meaning that HTTPS traffic share had fallen), as shown in the graphs below.

Further analysis also found the share of [_TCP connections terminated in the Post SYN stage_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/tcp-resets-timeouts/#sources-of-anomalous-connections) doubled during the observed outage, from 39% to 78%, as shown below. The cause of these unusual observations was ultimately uncovered by a [_Great Firewall Report blog post_](https://gfw.report/blog/gfw_unconditional_rst_20250820/en/), which stated, in part: “ _Between approximately 00:34 and 01:48 (Beijing Time, UTC+8) on August 20, 2025, the Great Firewall of China (GFW) exhibited anomalous behavior by unconditionally injecting forged TCP RST+ACK packets to disrupt all connections on TCP port 443. This incident caused massive disruption of the Internet connections between China and the rest of the world. … The responsible device does not match the fingerprints of any known GFW devices, suggesting that**the incident was caused by either a new GFW device or a known device operating in a novel or misconfigured state**._” This explanation is consistent with the anomalies visible in the Radar graphs.

### Pakistan

Subscribers of [_Nayatel (AS23674)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as23674) experienced an approximately 90 minute disruption to Internet connectivity on September 24, due to a [_reported outage at an upstream provider_](https://x.com/nayatelpk/status/1970791157404954809). Traffic dropped as much as 57% between around 09:15-10:45 UTC (14:15-15:45 local). [_Transworld (AS38193)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as38193) is one of several [_upstream providers_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as23674?dateStart=2025-09-24&dateEnd=2025-09-24#connectivity) to Nayatel, and a more significant drop in traffic is visible for that network, lasting from around 09:15-12:15 UTC (14:15-17:15 local time). The Nayatel disruption was likely less significant than the one seen at Transworld because Transworld is upstream of only a portion of the prefixes originated by Nayatel — traffic from other Nayatel prefixes was carried by other providers that remained available.

## No definitive cause

### Iran

Several weeks after experiencing a [_full Internet shutdown_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q2-2025-internet-disruption-summary/#iran), [_Iran_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/ir) again experienced a sudden drop in Internet traffic around 21:00 UTC on July 5 (00:30 local time on July 6), with [_traffic falling 80%_](https://x.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1941640046005617038) as compared to the prior week. While most of the “unknown” disruptions covered in this series of posts are observed but have no associated acknowledgement or explanation, this disruption had multiple competing explanations.

A [_published report_](https://www.iranintl.com/en/202507067645) noted “ _IRNA, Iran’s official news agency, cited the state-run Telecommunications Infrastructure Company, reporting a national-level disruption in international connectivity that affected most internet service providers Saturday night. Yet government officials have not publicly addressed the cause._ ” However, posts from civil society groups that follow Internet connectivity in Iran ([_net4people_](https://github.com/net4people/bbs/issues/497), [_FilterWatch_](https://x.com/filterbaan/status/1941628644125724793)) suggested that the disruption was again due to an intentional shutdown. And a [_post thread on X_](https://x.com/filterbaan/status/1941628644125724793) referenced, and disputed, a claim that the disruption was due to a DDoS attack. Unfortunately, no definitive root cause for this disruption could be found.

### Colombia

Customers of Claro Colombia experienced an Internet disruption that lasted just over 30 minutes on August 6, with [_traffic falling two-thirds or more_](https://x.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1953168943423864954) as compared to the prior week between 16:45 - 17:20 UTC. The disruption affected multiple ASNs owned by Claro, including [_AS10620_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as10620), [_AS14080_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as14080), and [_AS26611_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as26611). ### Pakistan

A near-complete outage at [_Pakistani_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/pk) backbone provider [_PTCL (AS17557)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as17557) caused traffic from the network provider to drop 90% at 16:10 UTC (21:10 local time) on August 19. PTCL acknowledged the issue in a [_post on X_](https://x.com/PTCLOfficial/status/1957873019084255347), noting “ _We are currently facing data connectivity challenges on our PTCL and Ufone services._ ” Although they [_published a subsequent post_](https://x.com/PTCLOfficial/status/1957977425377391076) several hours later after service was restored, they did not provide any additional information about the cause of the outage. However, [_one published report_](https://bloompakistan.com/nationwide-internet-disruption-hits-pakistan-ptcl-ufone-nayatel-services-severely-affected/) claimed “ _The disruption was primarily caused by a technical fault in PTCL’s fiber optic infrastructure._ ” while [_another report_](https://bloompakistan.com/nationwide-internet-disruption-hits-pakistan-ptcl-ufone-nayatel-services-severely-affected/) claimed “ _According to industry sources, the internet disruption in Pakistan may be connected to a technical fault in the fiber optic backbone or issues with main internet providers responsible for international online traffic._

Interestingly, [_traffic from PTCL to Cloudflare’s 1.1.1.1 DNS resolver_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/dns/as17557?dateStart=2025-08-19&dateEnd=2025-08-19#dns-query-volume) spiked as the outage began, and the [_share of requests made over UDP_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/dns/as17557?dateStart=2025-08-19&dateEnd=2025-08-19#dns-transport-protocol) grew from 94% to 99%. In addition, [_routing data_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as17557?dateStart=2025-08-19&dateEnd=2025-08-19) shows that there was also a small drop in announced IPv4 address space coincident with the outage. However, these additional observations do not necessarily confirm a “technical fault in PTCL’s fiber optic infrastructure” as the ultimate cause of the disruption.

### South Africa

To their credit, [_South African_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/za) provider [_RSAWEB (AS37053)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as37053) [_quickly acknowledged an issue_](https://netnotice.rsaweb.co.za/cmfe4mzqc0001ngqrbyfq0waj) with their FTTx and Enterprise connectivity on September 10, but neither their initial post nor subsequent updates provided any information on the cause of the problem. Whatever the cause, it resulted in a near-complete loss of Internet traffic from RSAWEB between 15:00 and 16:30 UTC (17:00 - 18:30 local time).

Routing data also shows a loss of just two announced /24 address blocks concurrent with the outage, dropping from 470 to 468. Unless all of RSAWEB’s outbound traffic was flowing through this limited amount of IP address space, it seems unusual that the withdrawal of just 512 IPv4 addresses from the=e routing table would have such a significant impact on the network’s traffic.

### SpaceX Starlink

After experiencing a _brief disruption in July_ due to a software failure, [_Starlink (AS14593)_](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as14593) suffered another short disruption between 04:00-05:00 UTC on September 15. Although Starlink generally acknowledges disruptions to their global network on [_their X account_](https://x.com/Starlink), and often providing a root cause, in this case they [_apparently published an acknowledgement_](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/starlink-suffers-brief-monday-outage-globally/) on X, but deleted it after the issue was resolved. In addition to the drop in traffic, we observed a concurrent drop in announced IPv4 address space and spike in BGP announcements (likely withdrawals), suggesting that the disruption may have been caused by a network-related issue.

Cloudflare’s network spans more than 310 cities in over 120 countries, where we interconnect with over 13,000 network providers in order to provide a broad range of services to millions of customers. The breadth of both our network and our customer base provides us with a unique perspective on Internet resilience, enabling us to observe the impact of Internet disruptions. Thanks to recently released Cloudflare Radar [functionality](https://developers.cloudflare.com/radar/glossary/#bgp-announcements), this quarter we have started to explore the impact from a routing perspective, as well as a traffic perspective, at both a [network](https://twitter.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1768654743742579059) and [location](https://twitter.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1773704264650543416) level.

The first quarter of 2024 kicked off with quite a few Internet disruptions. Damage to both terrestrial and submarine cables caused problems in a number of locations, while military action related to ongoing geopolitical conflicts impacted connectivity in other areas. Governments in several African countries, as well as Pakistan, ordered Internet shutdowns, focusing heavily on mobile connectivity. Malicious actors known as [Anonymous Suda](https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/ddos/glossary/anonymous-sudan/)n claimed responsibility for cyberattacks that disrupted Internet connectivity in Israel and Bahrain. Maintenance and power outages forced users offline, resulting in observed drops in traffic. And in a more unusual turn, RPKI, DNS, and DNSSEC issues were among the technical problems that disrupted connectivity for subscribers across multiple network providers.

As we have noted in the past, this post is intended as a summary overview of observed disruptions, and is not an exhaustive or complete list of issues that have occurred during the quarter.

## Cable cuts

### Moov Africa Tchad

Reported fiber optic cable damage that occurred in [Cameroon](https://radar.cloudflare.com/cm) on January 10 further disrupted connectivity for customers of [AS327802 (Moov Africa Tchad / Millicom)](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as327802) a telecommunications provider in Chad. According to a (translated) [Facebook post from Moov Africa Tchad](https://www.facebook.com/moovafrica.td/posts/pfbid0kB9W5CkhVJqBq34agPWqG81yeCfLBijKYc6WiLDKLE79nPmhie4T9idZVStc8f6Xl), “ _On the afternoon of January 10, 2024, there was a breakdown of the internet due to a cut in the optical fiber coming from Cameroon through which Chad has access to the internet, the one coming from Sudan being unavailable for a while._ ” It is unclear whether the referenced cable cut occurred in Cameroon or Chad, and the mentioned [Sudan](https://radar.cloudflare.com/sd) cable issue may be [the one covered in our Q4 2023 summary post](https://blog.cloudflare.com/q4-2023-internet-disruption-summary#:~:text=of%20Internet%20service.-,Chad,-On). As a landlocked country, Chad is dependent on terrestrial Internet connections to/through neighboring countries, and the [AfTerFibre cable map](https://afterfibre.nsrc.org/) illustrates Chad’s reliance on limited cable paths through Cameroon and Sudan.

The graphs below show that Moov Africa Tchad traffic was disrupted for over 12 hours starting midday (UTC) on January 10, and the disruption was visible at a country level as well. The fiber cut also resulted in significant volatility from a routing perspective, as the volume of announced IPv4 address space shifted frequently at a [network](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as327802?dateStart=2024-01-10&dateEnd=2024-01-11) and [country](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/td?dateStart=2024-01-10&dateEnd=2024-01-11) level during the disruption.

A second less severe disruption was also observed during the morning (UTC) of January 11. That disruption was [reportedly](https://twitter.com/DarkWebDispatch/status/1745080174519923165) due to an [alleged cyberattack](https://crisis24.garda.com/alerts/2024/01/chad-residual-internet-service-disruptions-possible-over-coming-hours-following-hours-long-outage-across-country-jan-10) by Anonymous Sudan that targeted [AS328594 (SudaChad Telecom)](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as328594), which is an [upstream provider for Moov Africa Tchad](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as327802).

### Orange Burkina Faso

On February 15, a brief (~30 minute) but complete significant Internet disruption was observed at [AS37577 (Orange Burkina Faso)](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as37577). According to the translation of a communiqué [posted by the provider on social media](https://twitter.com/OrangeBurkina/status/1758179417065464103), “ _The incident is due to a fiber cut, which causes a disruption of Internet services for certain customers._ ” Orange did not specify whether it was a more localized fiber cut, or damage to one of the terrestrial fibers that cross the country. The incident took the network completely offline, as the ASN’s amount of announced IPv4 address space [dropped to zero](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as37577?dateStart=2024-02-15&dateEnd=2024-02-15) for the duration.

### MTN Nigeria

[MTN Nigeria turned to social media](https://twitter.com/MTNNG/status/1762909655620063594) on February 28 to let customers know that “ _You have been experiencing challenges connecting to the network due to a major service outage caused by multiple fibre cuts, affecting voice and data services._ ” A [published report](https://crisis24.garda.com/alerts/2024/02/nigeria-lingering-internet-and-telecommunications-disruptions-likely-in-areas-across-country-following-nationwide-outage-feb-28) described the impact, noting “ _Millions of customers nationwide were impacted by the hours-long outage, especially in Lagos._ ” Connectivity was disrupted for approximately seven hours between 13:30 - 20:30 local time (12:30 - 19:30 UTC), and the provider [posted a followup note](https://twitter.com/MTNNG/status/1762972229195702692) just before midnight local time stating that service had been fully restored.

### Digicel Haiti

A 16-hour Internet disruption on March 2/3 at [AS27653 (Digicel Haiti)](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as27653) was due to a double fiber cut [as a result of violence](https://www.reuters.com/world/americas/haitian-police-unions-plead-help-after-attack-main-prison-2024-03-03/) related to attempts to oust Prime Minister Ariel Henry. Starting around 22:00 local time on March 2 (03:00 on March 3), a complete outage was observed for approximately nine hours. Some recovery in traffic occurred for approximately two-and-a-half hours, followed by a three hour near-complete disruption. Digicel Haiti effectively disappeared from the Internet during the nine-hour outage, as no IPv4 or IPv6 address space was announced by the network during that time.

### SKY (Philippines)

A brief traffic disruption observed on [AS23944 (SKY)](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as23944) in the [Philippines](https://radar.cloudflare.com/ph) on March 18 was likely related to a fiber cut. In an [advisory posted by SKY](https://twitter.com/SKYserves/status/1769688520124227960) on social media, they stated that “ _SKY services in several areas in Marikina, Pasig and Quezon City are currently affected by a cut-fiber issue_ ”, listing 45 affected areas. Traffic was most significantly impacted between 20:00 - 21:00 local time (12:00 - 13:00 UTC), although full recovery took several more hours. Only a [minor impact to routing](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as23944?dateStart=2024-03-18&dateEnd=2024-03-18) resulting from the fiber cut was observed.

### Multiple African countries

On March 14, damage to multiple submarine cables off the west coast of Africa impacted Internet connectivity across multiple countries in West and Southern Africa. The damage was [reportedly](https://www.itweb.co.za/article/news-analysis-why-undersea-cables-broke-sas-public-cloud/G98Yd7LGK4pvX2PD) caused by underwater rock falls, and in addition to disrupting Internet connectivity, also [caused availability issues for Microsoft Azure and Office 365 cloud services](https://web.archive.org/web/20240314144626/https://status.cloud.microsoft/).

The [Africa Coast to Europe (ACE)](https://www.submarinecablemap.com/submarine-cable/africa-coast-to-europe-ace), [Submarine Atlantic 3/West Africa Submarine Cable (SAT-3/WASC)](https://www.submarinecablemap.com/submarine-cable/sat-3wasc), [West Africa Cable System (WACS)](https://www.submarinecablemap.com/submarine-cable/west-africa-cable-system-wacs), and [MainOne](https://www.submarinecablemap.com/submarine-cable/mainone) cables [were all damaged](https://www.internetsociety.org/resources/doc/2024/2024-west-africa-submarine-cable-outage-report/), and impacted 13 African countries including [Benin](https://radar.cloudflare.com/bj), [Burkina Faso](https://radar.cloudflare.com/bf), [Cameroon](https://radar.cloudflare.com/cm), [Côte d’Ivoire](https://radar.cloudflare.com/ci), [Gambia](https://radar.cloudflare.com/gm), [Ghana](https://radar.cloudflare.com/gh), [Guinea](https://radar.cloudflare.com/gn), [Liberia](https://radar.cloudflare.com/lr), [Namibia](https://radar.cloudflare.com/na), [Niger](http://radar.cloudflare.com/ne), [Nigeria](https://radar.cloudflare.com/ng), [South Africa](https://radar.cloudflare.com/za), and [Togo](https://radar.cloudflare.com/tg).

Comparatively brief disruptions were observed in [Niger](https://radar.cloudflare.com/ne?dateStart=2024-03-14&dateEnd=2024-03-14), [Guinea](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/gn?dateStart=2024-03-14&dateEnd=2024-03-14), and [Gambia](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/gm?dateStart=2024-03-14&dateEnd=2024-03-14), lasting from under an hour to approximately two hours.

However, the disruptions stretched out across multiple days in countries including [Togo](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/tg?dateStart=2024-03-11&dateEnd=2024-03-17), [Liberia](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/lr?dateStart=2024-03-11&dateEnd=2024-03-17), and [Ghana](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/gh?dateStart=2024-03-11&dateEnd=2024-03-31), where it took several weeks for traffic to return to previously observed peak levels.

Operators in impacted countries attempted to maintain availability by shifting traffic to Google’s [Equiano](https://www.submarinecablemap.com/submarine-cable/equiano) submarine cable, which [reportedly](https://www.capacitymedia.com/article/2czls1jc2s3jtornuuhvk/news/exclusive-equianos-togo-landing-station-sees-4x-increase-in-traffic) experienced a 4x increase in traffic, and [Morocco’s](https://www.moroccoworldnews.com/2024/03/361490/west-africa-internet-outage-highlights-moroccos-crucial-role-in-regional-connectivity) [Maroc Telecom West Africa](https://www.submarinecablemap.com/submarine-cable/maroc-telecom-west-africa) submarine cable. Service on the [SAT-3](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/philippe-devaux-218423199_08apr24-west-africa-multiple-cables-outages-activity-7183050367440441344-iyTI?utm_source=share&utm_medium=member_desktop) cable was fully restored as of April 6, with repairs on [ACE](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/philippe-devaux-218423199_wacsabrcable-sat3abrcable-activity-7187430036432408576-404i?utm_source=share&utm_medium=member_desktop) completed on April 17, repairs to [WACS](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7187430036432408576/) and [MainOne](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/philippe-devaux-218423199_wacsabrcable-sat3abrcable-activity-7185980538434801664-dfa3?utm_source=share&utm_medium=member_desktop) expected to be done by April 28.

Additional details and observations can be found in our blog post [_Undersea cable failures cause Internet disruptions for multiple African countries_](https://blog.cloudflare.com/undersea-cable-failures-cause-internet-disruptions-across-africa-march-14-2024/).

### Red Sea

On February 24, three submarine cables that run through the Red Sea were damaged: the [Seacom/Tata cable](https://www.submarinecablemap.com/submarine-cable/seacomtata-tgn-eurasia), the [Asia Africa Europe-1](https://www.submarinecablemap.com/submarine-cable/asia-africa-europe-1-aae-1) (AAE-1), and the [Europe India Gateway](https://www.submarinecablemap.com/submarine-cable/europe-india-gateway-eig) (EIG). It is [believed](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-03-06/anchor-from-houthi-sunk-ship-likely-damaged-undersea-cables) that the cables were cut by the anchor of the [Rubymar](https://www.wired.com/story/houthi-internet-cables-ship-anchor-path/), a cargo ship that was damaged by a ballistic missile on February 18. At the time of the disruption, Seacom [confirmed](https://www.itweb.co.za/article/seacom-confirms-cable-outage-in-red-sea/KPNG8v8NyDNM4mwD) the damage to their cable, while the owners of the other two cables did not publish similar confirmations.

While the cable cuts [reportedly](https://www.wired.com/story/houthi-internet-cables-ship-anchor-path/#:~:text=impacted%20countries%20in%20East%20Africa) impacted countries in East Africa, including [Tanzania](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/tz?dateStart=2024-02-22&dateEnd=2024-02-28), [Kenya](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/ke?dateStart=2024-02-22&dateEnd=2024-02-28), [Uganda](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/ug?dateStart=2024-02-22&dateEnd=2024-02-28), and [Mozambique](https://radar.cloudflare.com/traffic/mz?dateStart=2024-02-22&dateEnd=2024-02-28), no loss of traffic was observed across these countries in [Cloudflare Radar](https://radar.cloudflare.com/).

## Military action

### Sudan

On February 2, Cloudflare observed a loss of traffic at [AS15706 (Sudatel)](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as15706) and [AS36972 (MTN Sudan)](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as36972), with a similar loss occurring on February 7 at [AS36998 (Zain Sudan / SDN Mobitel)](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as36998). The disruption at MTN Sudan aligns with a [social media post](https://twitter.com/MTNSudan1/status/1753528636949274956) from the provider, in which they stated (translated) “ _We regret the interruption of all services due to circumstances beyond our control. While we apologize for the inconvenience caused by this interruption, we assure you of our endeavor to restore the service as soon as possible, and you will be notified of the return of the service._ ” On February 5, several days after their outage started, Zain Sudan published a [social media post](https://twitter.com/ZainSudan/status/1754487740102533170) that stated (translated) “ _Zain Sudan has been constantly striving to maintain communication and Internet service to serve its valued subscribers, and we would like to point out that the current network outage is due to circumstances beyond its control, with our hopes that safety will prevail, and that service will be restored as soon as possible._ ” Sudatel did not share any information about the status of its network. On February 4, Digital Rights Lab - Sudan [posted on social media](https://twitter.com/drlab_sudan/status/1754134933772128326) that “ _Our sources confirmed that_ [_@RSFSudan_](https://twitter.com/RSFSudan) _forces tookover data centers of ISPs in Khartoum, #Sudan._ ” It is likely that the Internet outages observed across these providers are related to these takeovers, part of the [military conflict that has been underway in the country](https://www.cfr.org/global-conflict-tracker/conflict/power-struggle-sudan) since April 15, 2023.

The disruptions on these networks varied in length. At Sudatel, traffic started to return on February 11. At Zain Sudan, traffic began to return on March 3, corroborated by a [social media post](https://twitter.com/ZainSudan/status/1764217865530376300) that stated (translated) “ _Zain network is gradually returning to work and allows its subscribers to communicate for free for a limited time. Zain promises to continue working to restore its network in the rest of the states._ ” Traffic had not yet returned on MTN Sudan by the end of the first quarter.

### Orange España

Network [routing](https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/network-layer/what-is-routing/) is the process of selecting a path across one or more networks, and on the Internet, routing relies on the [Border Gateway Protocol (BGP)](https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/security/glossary/what-is-bgp/). Historically, the exchange of BGP routing information was based on trust between providers, but over time, security mechanisms such as [Resource Public Key Infrastructure (RPKI)](https://blog.cloudflare.com/rpki/) have been developed to prevent abuse of the system by bad actors. RPKI is a cryptographic method of signing records that associate a BGP route announcement with the correct originating AS number. ROA (Route Origin Authorization) records provide a means of verifying that an IP address block holder has authorized an [AS (Autonomous System)](https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/network-layer/what-is-an-autonomous-system/) to originate routes to that one or more prefixes within the address block. Cloudflare has [published a number of blog posts](https://blog.cloudflare.com/tag/rpki) over the years about the importance of, and our support for, RPKI. Properly implemented and configured, RPKI and ROAs help support routing security, effectively preventing behavior like [BGP hijacking](https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/security/glossary/bgp-hijacking/).

The [RIPE NCC](https://www.ripe.net/) (“RIPE”) is one of five [Regional Internet Registries (RIRs)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_Internet_registry) that provides Internet resource allocation and registration, and coordination activities. RIPE’s region covers Europe, the Middle East, and Central Asia. On January 3, a malicious actor took advantage of lax account security on the part of RIPE and [AS12479 (Orange España)](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as12479) and [used credentials found on the public Internet](https://blog.benjojo.co.uk/post/rpki-signed-but-not-secure) to log into Orange España’s RIPE account. Once in control of the account, the attacker published multiple ROAs with “bogus” origins, rendering thousands of routes originated by AS12479 “RPKI-invalid”, which [resulted in carriers that reject RPKI-invalid routes to stop carrying a large amount of Orange España’s IP space](https://www.kentik.com/blog/digging-into-the-orange-espana-hack/).

Because Cloudflare enforces RPKI validation, we also rejected the RPKI-invalid routes. We would have started trying to reach Orange España over our default route toward some of our transit providers, but because they also perform RPKI validation, traffic would have been dropped within those provider networks as well. Because of this, from Cloudflare’s perspective, this incident caused a drop in traffic from Orange España between 16:45 and 19:45 local time (14:45 - 17:45 UTC) as well as a [notable drop in announced IPv4 address space from AS12479](https://radar.cloudflare.com/routing/as12479?dateStart=2024-01-03&dateEnd=2024-01-03).

Orange España [confirmed on social media](https://twitter.com/orange_es/status/1742616775647265035) that its RIPE account had been improperly accessed, and as a result of the incident, [RIPE has made two-factor authentication (2FA) mandatory](https://www.ripe.net/publications/news/mandatory-2fa-on-ripe-ncc-access-accounts/) for logins. For additional insights into the incident, [Doug Madory at Kentik](https://www.kentik.com/blog/digging-into-the-orange-espana-hack/) and [Ben Cartwright-Cox at bgp.tools](https://blog.benjojo.co.uk/post/rpki-signed-but-not-secure) have both published detailed analyses and timelines.

### Russia

[DNS issues](https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/dns/common-dns-issues/) also impacted users in Russia during January, though in a different way than Plusnet subscribers in the UK experienced. A [reported](https://circleid.com/posts/20240130-dnssec-failure-causes-massive-website-outages-on-russian-internet) [DNSSEC](https://www.cloudflare.com/dns/dnssec/how-dnssec-works/) failure on January 30 resulted in .ru domains becoming inaccessible for several hours. (DNSSEC creates a secure domain name system by adding cryptographic signatures to existing DNS records. By checking its associated signature, you can verify that a requested DNS record comes from its authoritative name server and wasn’t altered en-route, as opposed to a fake record injected in a man-in-the-middle attack.)

The [DNSSEC validation failure](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc8914#name-extended-dns-error-code-6-d) resulted in [SERVFAIL](https://blog.cloudflare.com/unwrap-the-servfail) responses to DNS lookups against Cloudflare’s 1.1.1.1 resolver for hostnames in the .ru country code top level domain (ccTLD). At peak, 68.4% of requests received SERVFAIL responses. The Coordination Center for the .ru ccTLD [confirmed](https://t.me/fontankaspb/51445) that it was working on the “technical problem affecting the .ru zone associated with the global DNSSEC infrastructure” but didn’t provide any additional details around the root cause of the problem, such as a potential issue with a DNSSEC key rollover. The .ru ccTLD experienced a [similar DNSSEC-related outage](https://ianix.com/pub/dnssec-outages/20190816-ru/) for several hours on August 16, 2019, as well.

### AT&T (United States)

Starting just before 04:00 Eastern / 03:00 Central (09:00 UTC) on February 22, AT&T subscribers in several cities across the United States [experienced mobile service interruptions](https://abcnews.go.com/US/att-outage-impacting-us-customers-company/story?id=107440297). Impacted cities included Atlanta, Houston, and Chicago, with connectivity disrupted for approximately eight hours. Cloudflare data showed that as the problem began, [AT&T (AS7018)](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as7018) traffic [dropped as much as 45% in Chicago and 18% in Dallas](https://twitter.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1760694610689442114), as compared with the previous week.

According to a [“network update” published by AT&T](https://web.archive.org/web/20240223235440/https://about.att.com/pages/network-update), “ _Based on our initial review, we believe that today’s outage was caused by the application and execution of an incorrect process used as we were expanding our network, not a cyber attack._ ”

## Maintenance

### Vodafone Egypt

Between 05:15 and 11:30 local time (03:15 - 09:30 UTC) on March 5, customers of [AS36935 (Vodafone Egypt)](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as36935) experienced disruptions to their mobile Internet connectivity, with observed traffic from the network [dropping as much as 70% below expected levels](https://twitter.com/CloudflareRadar/status/1764951344954122467). A (translated) [social media post](https://twitter.com/VodafoneEgypt/status/1764947072376074428) from the provider noted in part “ _We apologize that some areas are currently affected by difficulties in operating the 4G service due to updates that took place this morning._ ” [As a result of the 4G network outage](https://www.africanwirelesscomms.com/news-details?itemid=7392&post=vodafone-egypt-sees-205-million-egp-fine-509046), Vodafone was required to compensate affected customers, and was also fined by [Egypt's National Telecommunications Regulatory Authority](https://www.tra.gov.eg/en/) (NTRA).

### Ocean Wave Communication (Myanmar)

Just before noon local time (05:15 UTC) on March 12, a significant drop in traffic was observed on [AS136442 (Ocean Wave)](https://radar.cloudflare.com/as136442), a consumer fiber and business Internet service provider in [Myanmar](https://radar.cloudflare.com/mm). A (translated) social media post from the provider noted “ _Ocean Wave customers, please be informed that there will be no internet/ slow connection due to network maintenance._ ” The connectivity disruption lasted approximately seven hours, with traffic returning to typical levels just before 19:00 local time (12:15 UTC).

## Conclusion

Two notable submarine cable damage events during the first quarter again highlighted the importance of protecting submarine cables, and the risks associated with them passing through/near geopolitically sensitive areas. Given the [reliance on submarine cables for carrying Internet traffic](https://blog.telegeography.com/2023-mythbusting-part-3), this will continue to be an issue for many years to come.

The Orange España incident also shed light on the importance of securing operationally important resources with [multi-factor authentication](https://www.cloudflare.com/learning/access-management/what-is-multi-factor-authentication/), a topic that Cloudflare has [written about in the past](https://blog.cloudflare.com/how-cloudflare-implemented-fido2-and-zero-trust). Organizations like RIPE play a critically important behind-the-scenes role in functioning of the Internet, arguably obligating them to take all practical precautions when it comes to securing their systems in order to prevent malicious actors from taking actions that could broadly disrupt Internet connectivity.

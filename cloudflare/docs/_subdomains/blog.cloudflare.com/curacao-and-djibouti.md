Cloudflare has just turned up two new datacenters (numbers 108 and 109). Both are around halfway between the [Tropic of Cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropic_of_Cancer) and the [Equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator). They are located continents-apart, yet share something very-much in common as both of these new data centers are deployed and associated with where undersea cables reach land. Undersea cables have been and still are a growing part of the interconnected world that the Internet represents.

### Curaçao, part of the Netherland Antilles in the Caribbean

[CC-BY 2.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0/) [image](https://flic.kr/p/FVVpmW) by [Nelo Hotsuma](https://www.flickr.com/photos/63122283@N06/)

Curaçao is located in the Southern Caribbean Sea (just north of Venezuela) and has a strong Dutch heritage. Along with Aruba and Bonaire, Curaçao is part of the Lesser Antilles (they are called the [ABC islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABC_islands_\(Lesser_Antilles\))).

More importantly, Willemstad - the capital of Curaçao is where the Amsterdam Internet Exchange operates [AMS-IX Caribbean](https://cw.ams-ix.net/connect-to-ams-ix-caribbean/benefits-of-connecting). Why AMS-IX? Because of that Dutch relationship!

It’s AMS-IX’s goal (along with its local partners) to promote Curaçao as an interconnection location for the Caribbean. Cloudflare is there with all its services ready for that day!

### Djibouti on the horn of Africa

Djibouti is a country of around 850,000 people with ~60% of the population living in the nation's capital, also called Djibouti. The country is about the same size as New Hampshire and its location (on the Horn of Africa) makes it a perfect location to bring ashore undersea fiber-optic cables.

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns%3D%22http%3A%2F%2Fwww.w3.org%2F2000%2Fsvg%22%20viewBox%3D%220%200%201%201%22%3E%3Crect%20width%3D%221%22%20height%3D%221%22%20fill%3D%22rgb\(243%2C243%2C243\)%22%2F%3E%3C%2Fsvg%3E)![Curaçao and Djibouti - two new Cloudflare data centers located where undersea cables meet Embedded Image - jaNC6j](https://blog.cloudflare.com/_image?href=https%3A%2F%2Fblog.cloudflare.com%2F_emdash%2Fapi%2Fmedia%2Ffile%2F01KW44SX0N43QVD6RVRM5PV6KE.jpg&w=715&h=402&f=webp&fit=cover&position=center)

Image of [landing the EASSy cable](https://wiocc.wordpress.com/2010/03/17/eassy-construction-two-thirds-complete/), Djibouti, Feb. 2010 courtesy of [WIOCC](http://wiocc.net/) & [EASSy](http://www.eassy.org/)

In fact, the number of undersea fiber-optic cables that have become active in the last five years along the coast of Africa is impressive. The African west coast has seen a boom and, the East African coast (where Djibouti is located) has seen even more of a boom with fiber-optic cables radiating outwards towards the Middle East, India, Asia, Southern Africa and Europe from Djibouti. The list of submarine fiber-optic cables that land at Djibouti seems to be increasing year-over-year. Recently the SeaMeWe-5 cable was brought into service. SeaMeWe stands for “South East Asia–Middle East–Western Europe”. It connects [Marseille France](https://blog.cloudflare.com/marseille/) (via a landing station in Toulon, France) through [Egypt](https://blog.cloudflare.com/cairo/), into Djibouti, then across to Sri Lanka and finaly finishing up in [Singapore](https://blog.cloudflare.com/cloudflares-singapore-data-center-now-online/). Interestingly, Cloudflare has data centers in four of those five locations.

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns%3D%22http%3A%2F%2Fwww.w3.org%2F2000%2Fsvg%22%20viewBox%3D%220%200%201%201%22%3E%3Crect%20width%3D%221%22%20height%3D%221%22%20fill%3D%22rgb\(243%2C243%2C243\)%22%2F%3E%3C%2Fsvg%3E)![Curaçao and Djibouti - two new Cloudflare data centers located where undersea cables meet Embedded Image - He4wzC](https://blog.cloudflare.com/_image?href=https%3A%2F%2Fblog.cloudflare.com%2F_emdash%2Fapi%2Fmedia%2Ffile%2F01KW45CJ5PKNZ2RHDFWHCM3M9F.png&w=715&h=402&f=webp&fit=cover&position=center)

[SeaMeWe-5](http://www.submarinecablemap.com/#/submarine-cable/seamewe-5) courtesy of [Submarine Cable Map](http://www.submarinecablemap.com/) from TeleGeography

Some of the existing undersea cables connecting Djibouti to the rest of the world include:

  * [Eastern Africa Submarine System (EASSy)](http://www.eassy.org/) connecting South Africa to Sudan via landing points including Djibouti.
  * [Europe India Gateway (EIG)](https://www.europeindiagateway.com/webclient/common/html/newsandevent.html) connnecting India and UAE via Djibouti to the UK.
  * [Middle East North Africa (MENA) Cable System/Gulf Bridge International](http://www.mena-scs.com/Index.aspx?PID=7) connecting Oman via Djibouti to Italy.
  * [SEACOM/Tata TGN-Eurasia](http://seacom.mu/network-map/) connecting South Africa, India, Djibouti, Egypt and onto Europe.
  * [SeaMeWe-3](http://www.submarinecablemap.com/#/submarine-cable/seamewe-3) connecting Asia, India, Middle East and Europe via Djibouti.
  * [SeaMeWe-5](http://www.seamewe5.com/route/swm5-maps/) connecting Asia, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Middle East and Europe via Djibouti.



The undersea cable business is always busy. There’s the following undersea cables being built, planned (or just dreamed about):

  * [Australia West Express (AWE)](http://australiawestexpress.net/) RFS Q2 2018

  * [Liquid Sea](http://www.submarinecablemap.com/#/submarine-cable/liquid-sea) RFC 2018



Djibouti is also a vital communications gateway for landlocked countries like Ethiopia and South Sudan. Landlocked countries are highly dependent on their path towards an ocean for access to the global Internet. That makes Djibouti a perfect location to provide Cloudflare services for specific areas of Eastern or Central Africa.

Right next to the landing station in Djibouti is the [Djibouti Internet Exchange](https://peeringdb.com/ix/967) (DjIX) which is located inside the [Djibouti Data Center](http://www.djiboutidatacenter.com/en/page/about) (DDC). They are separate buildings; however, highly interconnected. Cloudflare is a member of the DjIX and because of that internet exchange we expect to increase our connectivity up and down the East Coast of Africa.

If you’re interested to learn more about global undersea cables, look forward to a follow up deep dive (pun intended) blog post.

### Railways and fiber

Back around the start of the 20th century, the French built a one-meter gauge railway between the Ethiopian capital of Addis Ababa and the deep-water port in Djibouti. This old railway was recently disbanded and finally last year it was replaced by the modern electrified [Addis Ababa-Djibouti Railway](http://africa.cgtn.com/2017/01/10/44393/). This standard gauge international railway connects Addis Ababa and the Port of Doraleh in Djibouti. It’s used for freight, passengers and communications. The two railways are somewhat parallel and are the perfect path for fiber-optic cables linking Addis Ababa and Djibouti and hence connecting Ethiopia to the rest of the world. It’s very common for railways to be associated with fiber-optic cables. Ethiopia's single telecom company (Ethio telecom) is connected via Djibouti’s single telecom company (Djibouti Telecom).

### Undersea cables are the lifeblood of the global Internet

A long time ago the oceans were perceived as a barrier to efficient communications. Whilst they still represent significant distances and hence latency (the speed of light hasn’t changed as far as we can tell); they are not the economic barrier they once were. In fact, in some cases they can be very cost-effective at moving bits globally. Undersea cables have been pushing the barriers for Dense Wave Division Multiplexing (DWDM) and optical encoding for quite some time. A single fiber-optic cable can now encode data in the multi-Terabit range (16 or 32 Tbps is not complex to implement anymore). This eclipses anything that satellites can provide!

When an undersea cable surfaces (actually they are always buried, as they traverse the beaches on the ocean edges, before reaching the landing stations where the optical signal is converted into an electrical signal, as it ultimately reaches an Internet core router or Internet Exchange Point (IXP).

If you ever have the privilege of visiting a cable landing station, you’re in for a wonderful treat. Both the optical hardware and the power subsystems are normally some of the most interesting equipment in the Internet interconnection industry. Here’s [Andrew Blum](http://andrewblum.net/) giving a [TED talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/andrew_blum_what_is_the_internet_really) about undersea cables from his book Tubes; a great introduction to undersea cables.
